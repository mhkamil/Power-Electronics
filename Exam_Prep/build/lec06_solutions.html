Q1 · 2023 Q3(b): IGBT cross-section; PT vs NPT IGBT

Q. Draw the cross-section of an IGBT showing the layers. Differentiate between punch-through and non-punch-through IGBTs.

(a) Cross-section

Fig. 1: Cross-section of one IGBT cell (punch-through type). The NPT type has no n⁺ buffer layer.

Layers from collector to emitter:

  1. p⁺ substrate (collector): the injecting layer. It injects holes into the drift region when the device is on. This is what makes the IGBT different from a MOSFET (a MOSFET has n⁺ here).
  2. n⁺ buffer layer (PT only): stops the depletion region from reaching the p⁺ layer.
  3. n⁻ drift region: thick and lightly doped; it supports the blocking voltage.
  4. p body: the channel is formed in this layer under the gate.
  5. n⁺ emitter regions inside the p body, contacted by the emitter metal (the metal also shorts n⁺ to the p body).
  6. Gate: polysilicon over a thin SiO₂ layer, so the gate is insulated (very high input impedance).

Operation in one line: V_GE > V_th creates an n-channel in the p body, like a MOSFET. Electrons flow from the n⁺ emitter through the channel into the drift region; the p⁺ layer then injects holes. The drift region is flooded with carriers (conductivity modulation), so the on-state drop is low.

Q2 · 2022 Q6(d): Half-cell model of an IGBT [7]

Q. Briefly describe the half-cell model of an IGBT with appropriate figure.
Fig. 3a: Half cell with the parasitic devices.
Fig. 3b: Equivalent circuit of the half cell.

An IGBT chip is made of thousands of identical cells. Because each cell is symmetric, half a cell is enough to model the device. The half cell contains:

  1. MOSFET M1: n⁺ emitter, p body (channel) and n⁻ drift, controlled by the insulated gate.
  2. PNP transistor Q1: p⁺ collector (emitter of Q1), n⁻ drift (base of Q1), p body (collector of Q1). This is the main bipolar path.
  3. Parasitic NPN transistor Q2: n⁺ emitter, p body (base), n⁻ drift (collector).
  4. R_s: the lateral resistance of the p body under the n⁺ emitter, between the base of Q2 and the emitter contact.

Normal operation:

  • A gate voltage above V_th creates the channel. The MOSFET's electron current flows into the n⁻ drift, which is the base current of the PNP.
  • The PNP turns on and the p⁺ layer injects holes: conductivity modulation gives a low V_CE(sat).
  • So the IGBT behaves like a MOSFET driving a PNP transistor (the slide calls it a Darlington of an IGFET and a BJT): I_C = I_MOS + β_PNP·I_MOS = (1 + β_PNP)·I_MOS.
  • The holes collected by the p body flow to the emitter contact through R_s.

Latch-up:

  • If the hole current is too large, the drop across R_s exceeds about 0.7 V and forward-biases the n⁺–p junction, so the parasitic NPN turns on.
  • Q1 and Q2 then form a parasitic thyristor (p⁺n⁻pn⁺) that latches on, and the gate loses control; the device can be destroyed.
  • Prevention: keep R_s small (heavy p⁺ doping under the emitter, short n⁺ regions), short the n⁺ and p body with the emitter metal, and limit the current.

Q3 · 2022 Q6(b): Advantages of IGBT as a switch [4]

  1. Voltage-controlled insulated (MOS) gate: very high input impedance, negligible gate current, simple and cheap drive circuit.
  2. Low on-state voltage drop (like a BJT) because of conductivity modulation, so low conduction loss, much lower than a high-voltage MOSFET.
  3. High voltage and current ratings (up to several kV and kA) with high current density, so a smaller chip.
  4. Faster switching than a BJT; good for PWM inverters and drives (about 20–50 kHz).
  5. Turned on and off by the gate: no commutation circuit needed (unlike an SCR).
  6. Wide safe operating area; less prone to second breakdown than a BJT.
  7. NPT IGBTs have a positive temperature coefficient, so they are easy to parallel.
If the question also asks for disadvantages (slide): slower turn-off than a BJT or MOSFET because of the tail current; faster turn-off is possible only with a higher V_CE(sat); latch-up risk.

Q4 · 2021 Q3(d): Pinch-off condition in a power MOSFET [4]

Q. With diagram, explain the pinch-off condition in a power MOSFET.
Fig. 4: Left: channel pinch-off at the drain end. Right: output characteristics; the pinch-off locus separates the ohmic and active regions.
  1. Channel formation: for an n-channel enhancement MOSFET, a gate-source voltage V_GS above the threshold V_T induces an n-type inversion channel between source and drain.
  2. Ohmic region: for a small V_DS the channel has the same depth along its length and behaves like a resistor (R_DS(on)); I_D rises almost linearly with V_DS.
  3. Channel narrowing: as V_DS increases, the gate-to-channel voltage at the drain end, V_GD = V_GS − V_DS, decreases, so the channel becomes thinner near the drain.
  4. Pinch-off: when V_GD = V_T, that is
    V_DS = V_GS − V_T
    the channel depth at the drain end becomes zero. This is the pinch-off condition.
  5. Beyond pinch-off (V_DS > V_GS − V_T): the extra voltage is dropped across the small depletion region near the drain; the current stops increasing and stays at I_D ≈ K(V_GS − V_T)². The MOSFET is in the active (saturation) region, where I_D is controlled by V_GS only.
  6. The locus of pinch-off points, I_D = K·V_DS², is the boundary between the ohmic and active regions (dashed line in Fig. 4).

For switching, a power MOSFET is used in the ohmic region (ON) and cut-off (OFF); the active region is crossed only during transitions.

Likely new questions from this lecture (short answers)

PredictedQ. Write the features and disadvantages of a power MOSFET.
Features (slide): low input current; voltage-controlled; high switching speed; no second breakdown; high input impedance; unipolar; no commutation circuit needed; small size; small holding power.
Disadvantages (slide): high on-state voltage, so high conduction loss compared with a BJT; easily damaged by static electricity, so it needs careful handling; asymmetric blocking: blocks high forward voltage but not high reverse voltage.
PredictedQ. Why is a vertical (VDMOS) structure used for power MOSFETs?
In a lateral MOSFET both the current and the breakdown voltage depend on the channel dimensions, so it is inefficient. In the vertical, double-diffused MOSFET the source is on top and the drain at the bottom, so current flows vertically. The voltage rating depends on the doping and thickness of the n⁻ layer, while the current rating depends on the channel width. The device can therefore handle high voltage and high current without becoming large. "Double-diffused" refers to the p and n⁺ wells made by two diffusions.
PredictedQ. Why was the IGBT developed? / Explain the turn-off of a power MOSFET.
Why IGBT: a BJT needs a large base current (even as a Darlington); a MOSFET needs almost no gate current but has a high on-state drop at high current. The IGBT combines the MOSFET's insulated gate with the BJT's low on-state drop.
MOSFET turn-off (slide): t5: v_GS falls, C_GS discharges and C_GD charges; i_D and v_DS unchanged. t6: v_DS rises to V_CC while v_GS stays constant (Miller plateau); then the diode turns on and i_D starts to fall. t7: v_GS falls to V_T and i_D falls to zero (cut-off). t6 and t7 have both i_D and v_DS present, so they cause the switching loss.
