1. Dynamic (switching) characteristics

When a diode switches, its current and voltage do not change instantly. The slide divides the transition into intervals:

Fig. 1: Diode turn-on (t0, t1) and turn-off (t2, t3, t4). Shaded area = reverse recovery charge Q_RR.
IntervalWhat happens
t0 (turn-on)Source goes from negative to positive. Stored charge in the depletion region is removed; the current starts to rise. The diode voltage shows a peak (forward overshoot V_FP) because carrier injection is not yet complete and the N-region resistance is high.
t1Carrier injection completes and the voltage falls to the forward drop V_F. t0 and t1 are tens to hundreds of ns in power diodes. End of t1 = turn-on complete.
t2 (turn-off starts)Source goes from positive to negative. The minority carriers (electrons in P, holes in N) must be removed before the potential barrier forms, so the diode keeps conducting. The current falls at the rate di/dt; the voltage falls slightly as R_ON falls.
t3 (= t_a)The current goes negative and reaches its negative peak −I_RR. At this point the stored minority carriers have been swept out and the diode starts to become reverse-biased.
t4 (= t_b)The current returns to zero as the depletion region recharges. The diode voltage shows a reverse overshoot because of the circuit's parasitic (stray) inductance.
t_rrReverse recovery time t_rr = t3 + t4 (t_a + t_b). The area under the time axis in the current waveform is the reverse recovery charge Q_RR.

2. Reverse recovery (most important topic)

Definitions

  • Reverse recovery time (t_rr): the time from the instant the forward current crosses zero until the reverse current has decayed to about 25% of its peak I_RR (practically, back to zero); t_rr = t_a + t_b.
  • Peak reverse recovery current (I_RR): the maximum reverse current that flows during recovery, at the end of t_a.
  • Reverse recovery charge (Q_RR): the charge carried by the reverse current during recovery, equal to the area of the reverse-current triangle (shaded in Fig. 1).
  • t_a: time to remove stored charge from the depletion region. t_b: time to remove stored charge from the bulk semiconductor.
  • Softness factor S = t_b / t_a.

Formulas (and where they come from)

Approximate the reverse current as a triangle with height I_RR and base t_rr:
  Q_RR ≈ ½ · I_RR · t_rr ……(1)
The current falls at a constant rate di/dt during t_a:
  I_RR = t_a · (di/dt) ……(2)
For an abrupt recovery (t_b small), t_a ≈ t_rr, so I_RR ≈ t_rr · (di/dt). Substituting into (1):
  Q_RR = ½ · (di/dt) · t_rr² ……(3)
Rearranging (3) and combining with (2):
  t_rr = √(2 Q_RR / (di/dt)) ……(4)    I_RR = √(2 Q_RR · (di/dt)) ……(5)
Remember: t_rr and I_RR depend on the stored charge Q_RR and on di/dt (which the circuit sets). Q_RR grows with the forward current I_F.

Significance (importance) of t_rr

  1. Sets the maximum switching frequency. Reverse recovery must finish before the diode is forward-biased again. The lower t_rr, the faster the diode can be switched (fast / ultrafast diodes).
  2. Switching losses. During t_rr the diode carries reverse current while voltage builds up across it, so energy is lost each cycle. Loss rises with frequency.
  3. Stress on the other switch. I_RR adds to the current of the transistor that turns on, increasing its peak current and turn-on loss.
  4. Overvoltage and EMI. A fast fall of reverse current through stray inductance gives v = L·di/dt spikes, overvoltage and electromagnetic interference.
  5. Choice of diode. Rectifiers at line frequency can use general-purpose diodes; choppers and inverters need fast-recovery or Schottky diodes.

3. Types of diodes

Common types (slide 6)

TypeKey points
Small-signal diodeLow voltage, low current; low-power rectifiers, limiters, amplifiers, waveshaping. Designer must know V_F, breakdown voltage, I_RR and t_rr.
Power rectifier diodeHigh forward current and high reverse breakdown voltage; rectifiers, inverters, power supplies. Thermal resistance matters; usually needs a heat sink.
Zener diodeVoltage regulation; operates in reverse breakdown; the Zener current clamps the voltage.
Schottky diodeThin metal film on the semiconductor gives a rectifying contact. V_F ≈ 0.3 V; low-voltage applications.

Why Schottky recovers so fast: conduction is by majority carriers only, so there is no stored minority charge. The small recovered charge comes from the junction capacitance only, so it is largely independent of the reverse di/dt.

Silicon carbide (SiC) diodes

  • SiC outperforms Si and GaAs. SiC Schottky diodes have no reverse recovery time, ultrafast switching, and switching that is not affected by temperature.
  • Benefits: lowest switching losses (low Q_RR), surge-current stable and rugged, lower cooling cost, higher frequency and power density, low device capacitance, less EMI.
  • Uses: power supplies, solar inverters, transport, welding, air conditioners.
  • A Schottky diode is made by joining doped n-type semiconductor with a metal such as gold, silver or platinum.

4. Series and parallel connection

Fig. 3: (a) Series diodes with voltage-sharing resistors. (b) Parallel diodes with current-sharing resistors (or equal inductances).

Series-connected diodes (for high voltage, e.g. HVDC)

  • Why: one diode cannot block the required voltage.
  • Problem: diodes of the same type have different v-i curves (production tolerance). In reverse blocking they all carry the same leakage current, so their blocking voltages can be very different; one diode may break down.
  • Solution: connect a resistor across each diode to force equal voltage sharing. The leakage currents of the diodes then differ, but each diode-plus-resistor pair carries the same total current. (Capacitors are added across each diode to share voltage during transients.)
  • Equations: I_s1 + V_D1/R1 = I_s2 + V_D2/R2 and V_D1 + V_D2 = V_D.

Parallel-connected diodes (for high current)

  • Why: one diode cannot carry the required current.
  • Problem: current divides according to the forward voltage drops; the diode with the lowest V_F hogs the current.
  • Solution: equal series inductances (e.g. in the leads) or current-sharing resistors (the resistors waste power, so they may not be practical). Choosing diodes of the same type with equal V_F also helps.
  • In parallel, every diode sees the same reverse voltage.

5. Applications of diodes

  • Rectifiers (AC to DC) and voltage multipliers.
  • Freewheeling diode: gives the inductor current a path when the transistor turns off.
Fig. 4: Freewheeling diode D_FW across an inductive load.

Without D_FW, turning the switch off would force the inductor current to zero instantly. That gives a huge v = L·di/dt across the switch, which would destroy it. With D_FW, the inductor voltage reverses and forward-biases the diode, so the current continues smoothly through D_FW and the switch is protected.

6. Previous-year questions with full solutions

TT-1 2020Q. Explain the reverse recovery time of a diode with diagram. [4]
Answer. Draw Fig. 1 (current part is enough, label I_F, −I_RR, t_a, t_b, t_rr, Q_RR).
When a conducting diode is suddenly reverse-biased, the current does not stop at zero. The stored minority carriers in the junction and bulk must first be removed, so the current falls at the rate di/dt, passes through zero and flows in the reverse direction.
  • During t_a the charge in the depletion region is removed and the reverse current rises to its peak I_RR.
  • During t_b the charge in the bulk semiconductor is removed and the reverse current decays to zero.
  • t_rr = t_a + t_b is the reverse recovery time; the area of the reverse current is the reverse recovery charge Q_RR ≈ ½ I_RR t_rr.
The diode can block reverse voltage only after t_rr has ended.
2021 Q2(a)Q. Discuss the significance of reverse recovery time of a diode. [5]
Answer. Start with a one-line definition plus the current sketch (Fig. 1), then give the points:
  1. Maximum switching frequency: recovery must be over before the diode is forward-biased again, so t_rr limits how fast the diode can switch. Low t_rr means fast/ultrafast diodes.
  2. Switching loss: reverse current flows while reverse voltage builds up, so energy is lost every cycle; the loss is proportional to switching frequency.
  3. Extra stress on the complementary switch: the I_RR adds to the transistor current at turn-on (higher peak current, higher loss).
  4. Voltage spikes and EMI: the rapid fall of reverse current through stray inductance gives L·di/dt overvoltage and noise. Soft-recovery diodes reduce this.
  5. Diode selection: line-frequency rectifiers use general-purpose diodes (t_rr ≈ 25 µs); choppers and inverters need fast-recovery (< 5 µs) or Schottky diodes.
2023 Q1(b)Q. Define reverse recovery current and reverse recovery time for diode switching. What is the importance of these parameters? [6]
Answer.
  • Reverse recovery time t_rr: the time between the instant the forward current falls to zero and the instant the reverse current decays back to (almost) zero; t_rr = t_a + t_b.
  • Reverse recovery current I_RR: the peak reverse current that flows during recovery, at the end of t_a; I_RR = t_a·(di/dt) ≈ √(2Q_RR·di/dt).
Draw Fig. 1 (current waveform) with t_a, t_b, t_rr, I_RR and Q_RR marked.
Importance:
  1. t_rr fixes the highest usable switching frequency.
  2. I_RR and t_rr together set the reverse recovery loss (∝ Q_RR · V_R · f).
  3. I_RR flows through the switch that turns on, increasing its peak current and stress.
  4. The fall of I_RR through stray inductance causes overvoltage and EMI, which is why soft recovery is preferred.
  5. They decide which diode type to use (general-purpose, fast-recovery or Schottky).
2021 Q4(c) [4]2023 Q1(c) [6]Q. The manufacturer of a selected diode gives the rate of fall of the diode current di/dt = 20 A/µs, and its reverse recovery time t_rr = 5 µs. What value of peak reverse current (and storage charge) do you expect?
Given: di/dt = 20 A/µs = 20 × 10⁶ A/s, t_rr = 5 µs = 5 × 10⁻⁶ s.
Step 1: stored charge. Q_RR = ½ (di/dt) t_rr² = ½ × 20 × 10⁶ × (5 × 10⁻⁶)² = ½ × 20 × 10⁶ × 25 × 10⁻¹² = 250 µC
Step 2: peak reverse current. I_RR = √(2 Q_RR (di/dt)) = √(2 × 250 × 10⁻⁶ × 20 × 10⁶) = √10000 = 100 A
Check: I_RR = t_rr × di/dt = 5 µs × 20 A/µs = 100 A ✓
Answer: I_RR = 100 A, Q_RR = 250 µC
Assumption to write in the exam: abrupt recovery (t_b small), so t_a ≈ t_rr.
2022 Q1(a)(ii)Q. Write a short note on: Freewheeling diode. [about 4]
Answer. A freewheeling (flywheel) diode is connected across an inductive load (anti-parallel to the load), as in Fig. 4.
  • Purpose: when the switch (or the rectifier diode/SCR) turns off, the inductor current cannot stop instantly. The freewheeling diode gives it a closed path, so the current continues smoothly.
  • Protects the switch: without it, the inductor would create a very large L·di/dt voltage across the switch and destroy it.
  • In rectifiers (e.g. half-wave with RL load): it stops the output voltage going negative, so V_o increases (V_o = V_m/π), the load current becomes smoother (continuous for large L), and the input power factor improves. The rectifier diode no longer conducts in the negative half cycle.
  • Used in choppers (DC-DC converters), rectifiers with inductive loads, relay and motor drives.

7. Practice questions (likely next) with short answers

PredictedQ. A diode has Q_RR = 1500 µC and di/dt = 30 A/µs. Find t_rr and I_RR.
t_rr = √(2Q_RR/(di/dt)) = √(2 × 1500 × 10⁻⁶ / 30 × 10⁶) = 10 µs; I_RR = √(2Q_RR·di/dt) = √(2 × 1500 × 10⁻⁶ × 30 × 10⁶) = 300 A (check: 10 µs × 30 A/µs = 300 A ✓)
PredictedQ. Two diodes are connected in series to block V_D = 5 kV. Leakage currents I_s1 = 30 mA, I_s2 = 35 mA. (a) Find each diode voltage if R1 = R2 = 100 kΩ. (b) Find R1 for equal sharing if R2 = 100 kΩ.
(a) I_s1 + V_D1/R = I_s2 + V_D2/R with V_D2 = V_D − V_D1 ⇒ V_D1 = V_D/2 + R(I_s2 − I_s1)/2 = 2500 + 100 kΩ × 5 mA / 2 = 2750 V, V_D2 = 2250 V.
(b) V_D1 = V_D2 = 2500 V: I_s1 + 2500/R1 = I_s2 + 2500/R2 ⇒ 2500/R1 = 35 mA − 30 mA + 25 mA = 30 mA ⇒ R1 = 83.33 kΩ.
This textbook example (Rashid) is not on your slides, but the slide explains the method, so an examiner can ask it.
PredictedQ. Compare general-purpose, fast-recovery and Schottky diodes. / Why does a Schottky diode have almost no reverse recovery?
Use the table in Section 3. Schottky: majority-carrier device, so no stored minority charge; only junction capacitance gives a small recovered charge, independent of di/dt; low V_F (≈ 0.3 V) but low reverse voltage rating.
PredictedQ. What are soft and hard recovery? Which is preferred and why?
Soft recovery: slow fall of reverse current (large t_b, S = t_b/t_a large). Hard recovery: fast, snappy fall. Soft is preferred because the voltage induced in stray inductances (L·di/dt) is lower, so less overvoltage, oscillation and EMI.
PredictedQ. Why are resistors connected across series diodes and resistors/inductors in series with parallel diodes?
Series: diodes of the same type have different leakage characteristics, so with the same leakage current their reverse voltages differ; shunt resistors force equal voltage sharing. Parallel: current divides according to V_F, so the diode with the lowest V_F carries most current; equal series resistors or lead inductances force equal current sharing.
