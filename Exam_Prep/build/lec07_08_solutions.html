Q1 · Two-transistor model of a thyristor

2021 Q2(b) [8] · 2023 Q2(a) [7]: Draw the two-transistor model of a thyristor and explain the turn-on condition with equations from this model.
2022 Q4(c) [6]: Using the two-transistor model explain the anode current in a thyristor. · 2024 Q1(a) [7]: With a two-transistor model explain the characteristics of an SCR.
Fig. 1: (a) The PNPN structure viewed as two interconnected transistors. (b) Equivalent circuit.

Model. The regenerative (latching) action of a thyristor can be shown by splitting its PNPN structure into two complementary transistors: a PNP transistor Q1 (p-n-p from the anode side) and an NPN transistor Q2 (n-p-n from the cathode side). The middle n and p layers are shared: the collector of each transistor drives the base of the other, which is positive feedback.

Derivation. For any transistor, I_C = αI_E + I_CBO, where α is the common-base current gain and I_CBO the collector-base leakage current.

For Q1 (emitter current = I_A):    I_C1 = α₁I_A + I_CBO1 ……(1)
For Q2 (emitter current = I_K):    I_C2 = α₂I_K + I_CBO2 ……(2)
Anode current (KCL at Q1):      I_A = I_C1 + I_C2 ……(3)
Cathode current:                    I_K = I_A + I_G ……(4)
Put (1), (2), (4) into (3):  I_A = α₁I_A + I_CBO1 + α₂(I_A + I_G) + I_CBO2
                            I_A(1 − α₁ − α₂) = α₂I_G + I_CBO1 + I_CBO2
                            I_A = (α₂I_G + I_CBO1 + I_CBO2) / [1 − (α₁ + α₂)] ……(5)

Turn-on condition (explaining equation 5).

  • α₁ and α₂ are not constant: they are small at low emitter current and increase as the current increases.
  • Off state (forward blocking): with I_G = 0 the currents are only leakage, so α₁ + α₂ is much less than 1 and I_A ≈ (I_CBO1 + I_CBO2), a tiny leakage current.
  • Gate current applied: I_G increases I_K, so α₂ rises; I_C2 rises, and since I_C2 is the base current of Q1, I_C1 rises; I_C1 is the base current of Q2, so I_C2 rises further. This is regenerative (positive feedback).
  • As the currents grow, α₁ + α₂ → 1, the denominator of (5) → 0, so I_A → ∞ in theory. In practice I_A is limited only by the external circuit: the thyristor has turned on (latched).
  • Once I_A is above the latching current I_L, the loop sustains itself and the gate signal can be removed. The SCR stays on until I_A falls below the holding current I_H.
  • Anything that raises α or the leakage currents (high temperature, high V_AK near V_BO, a large dv/dt, light) can also make α₁ + α₂ reach 1, which is why these are the other turn-on methods.

For 2024 Q1(a) ("explain the characteristics"): add Fig. 2 and link each region to the model: forward blocking (α₁ + α₂ < 1, only leakage); breakover at V_BO (avalanche leakage raises α₁ + α₂ to 1); a larger I_G reaches α₁ + α₂ = 1 at a lower voltage, so V_BO falls; on-state (latched, about 1 V drop); I_L and I_H; reverse blocking (J1 and J3 reverse-biased).

Q2 · 2020 TT-1: I-V curve; holding and latching current [5]

Fig. 2: SCR I-V characteristics with the effect of gate current.
  • Forward blocking: anode positive; J1 and J3 forward-biased, J2 reverse-biased; only a small leakage (off-state) current flows.
  • Forward breakover V_BO: if V_AK is raised far enough, J2 breaks down by avalanche; all three junctions conduct and the SCR switches to the on-state with only about 1 V drop. In practice V_AK is kept below V_BO and the SCR is turned on by gate current; more gate current means a lower breakover voltage.
  • Reverse blocking: cathode positive; J1 and J3 reverse-biased (like two series diodes); only reverse leakage I_R flows, until reverse breakdown.
Latching current I_L: the minimum anode current required to keep the thyristor in the on-state immediately after it has been turned on and the gate signal has been removed.
Holding current I_H: the minimum anode current required to maintain the thyristor in the on-state. If I_A falls below I_H, a depletion region forms around J2 and the SCR returns to the blocking state. It is of the order of milliamperes.
I_L > I_H.

Q3 · 2021 TT-1: Five turn-on processes; effect of dv/dt [5]

Five ways to turn on a thyristor (slide):
  1. Thermal: high temperature raises electron-hole pairs and leakage, so α₁ + α₂ rises toward 1. May cause thermal runaway; normally avoided.
  2. Light: light striking the junctions creates electron-hole pairs (light-activated SCRs, LASCR).
  3. High voltage: V_AK > V_BO; leakage starts regenerative turn-on. Can be destructive; avoided.
  4. dv/dt: a fast-rising V_AK charges the junction capacitance (below).
  5. Gate current: a positive gate-cathode voltage injects gate current; the normal, practical method. More gate current means a lower forward blocking voltage.
Fig. 3: Thyristor transient model: junction capacitances C_j1, C_j2, C_j3.

Effect of dv/dt: in forward blocking, J2 is reverse-biased and behaves like a capacitor C_j2. A rapidly rising anode voltage drives a charging current i_j2 = C_j2·dV_j2/dt (+ V_j2·dC_j2/dt) through it. This current acts like a gate current: it increases the leakage currents I_CBO1 and I_CBO2, so α₁ + α₂ can reach 1 and the SCR turns on without a gate signal (false triggering). A large charging current can also damage the device. Protection: an RC snubber across the SCR limits dv/dt; manufacturers specify the maximum allowable dv/dt.

Q4 · 2022 Q4(d): Methods of SCR turn-on and turn-off [8]

Turn-on methods

List and explain the five methods from Q3 (thermal, light, high voltage, dv/dt, gate current), stating that gate triggering is the practical method and the others are mostly unwanted. Add the dynamic turn-on picture:

Fig. 4: Dynamic turn-on: after the gate pulse, the anode current needs the delay time t_d and rise time t_r.

Gate-pulse rules (slide): remove the gate signal once the SCR is on (to save gate loss); do not apply a gate signal while the SCR is reverse-biased; the gate pulse must last longer than the time the anode current needs to reach the holding (latching) value, and in practice longer than t_on.

Turn-off methods (commutation)

The gate cannot turn an SCR off. Turn-off (commutation) needs two conditions: (1) the anode current must be reduced below the holding current I_H, and (2) a reverse voltage must be applied for a sufficient time so that the excess carriers are removed and the SCR regains forward blocking.

Fig. 5: Turn-off characteristics: t_q = t_rr + t_gr; the circuit must give t_c > t_q.
  • t_rr (t1 to t3): reverse recovery current removes carriers from the outer junctions J1, J3.
  • t_gr (t3 to t4): carriers at J2 disappear by recombination while reverse voltage is held. t_q = t_rr + t_gr (10 to 100 µs).
  • The commutation circuit must give a circuit turn-off time t_c > t_q, or commutation fails.
MethodHow the current is forced to zero
Natural (line, class F)The AC supply itself: current passes through a natural zero at the end of the positive half cycle and the supply then reverse-biases the SCR.
Class A (self / resonant / load)Load is an underdamped RLC circuit; the current oscillates to zero and the charged capacitor reverse-biases the SCR. Good above 1000 Hz.
Class B (self, resonant LC across SCR)An LC circuit across the SCR (not in series with load) rings and turns the SCR off periodically; used in choppers.
Class C (complementary)A second SCR in parallel; firing it puts a charged capacitor's reverse voltage across the main SCR. See Q6.
Class D (auxiliary / impulse)An auxiliary SCR switches a pre-charged capacitor across the main SCR; diode D and L reverse the capacitor polarity.
Class E (external pulse)A pulse transformer injects a reverse voltage pulse (lasting at least t_q) across the SCR.

Device grades (slide): converter-grade SCRs have t_q ≈ 50 to 100 µs (rectifiers, cycloconverters, AC regulators); inverter-grade SCRs have t_q ≈ 3 to 50 µs (choppers, inverters) and cost more.

Q5 · 2024 Q1(b): Natural vs forced commutation [6]

Q. Explain how natural commutation and forced commutation occur, giving one static power semiconductor device example for each.

Natural commutation (line / source / class F): the commutation voltage comes from the AC supply itself. At the end of every positive half cycle the anode current passes through a natural current zero, and immediately afterwards the supply applies a reverse voltage across the SCR. Both turn-off conditions are met, so the SCR turns off by itself.

Example: an SCR (thyristor) in a single-phase phase-controlled rectifier. Natural commutation is also used in AC voltage controllers (SCR or TRIAC), cycloconverters and line-commutated inverters.

Fig. 6: Natural commutation of an SCR (resistive load): the current ends at π and the supply then reverse-biases the SCR.

Forced commutation: in DC circuits there is no natural current zero. An external commutation circuit of L and C forces the anode current to zero and applies a reverse voltage across the SCR (Classes A to E).

Example: an SCR in a DC chopper (DC-DC converter) or in a forced-commutated inverter.

Q6 · 2021 Q3(c): One forced commutation circuit [6]

Q. Briefly describe any one kind of thyristor forced commutation circuit with necessary diagram and waveforms.

Chosen: Class C (complementary) commutation. The main SCR T₁ is in series with the load R₁; an auxiliary (complementary) SCR T₂ with R₂ is connected in parallel with it, and a capacitor C joins the two anodes.

Fig. 7a: Class C commutation circuit.
Fig. 7b: Waveforms (R₁ = R₂, steady state).
  1. T₁ fired: load current V_s/R₁ flows through R₁ and T₁. At the same time C charges through R₂ and T₁ until plate b is at +V_s (a at 0). i_T1 has an extra decaying capacitor-charging spike.
  2. T₂ fired (to turn T₁ off): point b drops to 0. Because a capacitor's voltage cannot change instantly, point a jumps to −V_s: the charged capacitor puts a reverse voltage across T₁, its current falls to zero and it turns off.
  3. C now charges through R₁ (and T₂) in the opposite direction: v_T1 = V_s(1 − 2e^(−t/R₁C)). T₁ stays reverse-biased until v_T1 crosses zero, which gives the circuit turn-off time
    t_c = R₁C·ln 2 = 0.693 R₁C (must be > t_q of T₁)
  4. T₁ fired again: a drops to 0, b jumps to −V_s, so T₂ is reverse-biased and turns off. The cycle repeats; each SCR turns the other off.

Features (slide): also called complementary commutation; the SCR turns off by the reverse voltage of a charged capacitor; very reliable; useful even below 1000 Hz; used in single-phase inverters with centre-tapped transformers.

Q7 · 2024 Q1(c): Minimum gate pulse width [7]

Q. The holding current of the thyristors in a single-phase full converter is I_H = 500 mA and the delay time is t_d = 1.5 µs. The converter is supplied from a 120 V, 60 Hz supply and has a load of L = 10 mH and R = 10 Ω. The converter is operated with a delay angle α = 30°. Determine the minimum value of gate pulse width t_G.
This is Example 14-1 on your Lecture 8 slides, word for word.
Step 1: supply voltage at the firing instant. V_m = √2 × 120 = 169.7 V; at ωt = α = 30°:
  V₁ = V_m sin α = 169.7 × sin 30° = 84.85 V
Step 2: initial rate of rise of anode current. Just after triggering the inductor limits the current (R·i is still small):
  di/dt = V₁/L = 84.85 / (10 × 10⁻³) = 8485 A/s
Step 3: time for the current to reach the holding value. Taking di/dt as constant for this short time:
  t₁ = I_H / (di/dt) = 0.5 / 8485 = 58.93 µs
Step 4: minimum gate pulse width. The gate must stay on during the delay time plus this rise time:
  t_G = t₁ + t_d = 58.93 + 1.5 = 60.43 µs
Answer: t_G(min) ≈ 60.43 µs (this is the slide's answer)
Check with the exact RL current i(t) = (V₁/R)(1 − e^(−tR/L)): t₁ = −(L/R)ln(1 − I_H R/V₁) = 60.73 µs, so t_G = 62.23 µs. Both are accepted; write the slide method and mention the assumption "di/dt constant". In practice t_G is made longer than t_on.

Q8 · Related questions answered in your Lecture 5 notes

  • 2024 Q1(d): why SCRs (and BJTs) cannot be paralleled easily: negative temperature coefficient, unequal on-state drops and turn-on times, so one device hogs the current. Needs series R/L and simultaneous strong gate pulses.
  • 2024 Q3(b): secondary breakdown occurs in thyristors because they are bipolar devices with negative temperature coefficient and regenerative action: current filaments form hot spots.

Thyristors vs transistors (slide): thyristors have lower conduction loss and higher power capability; transistors switch faster with lower switching loss.
