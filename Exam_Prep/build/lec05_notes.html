1. Introduction and basics

  • The BJT was the traditional power switch. IGBTs and MOSFETs have now largely replaced it, but BJTs are still used where cost is the main concern.
  • It is a current-controlled, bipolar (minority-carrier) device: a base current I_B is needed to keep it on (I_C = βI_B in the active region).

Basics of a power BJT (slide 2)

  • Larger current and power handling than a small-signal BJT.
  • Higher voltage blocking in the OFF state; higher current in the ON state.
  • Vertically oriented structure (current flows from top to bottom).
  • Emitter is very heavily doped compared with the base; even so, the current gain β is low (typically 5 to 20).
  • Large metal body (TO-3 style case) to dissipate heat.

2. Structure (constructional features)

Fig. 1: Vertical n⁺-p-n⁻-n⁺ structure of a power BJT (doping and thickness from the slide figure).
  • Four layers: n⁺ emitter, p base, n⁻ collector drift region, n⁺ substrate (collector contact).
  • Drift region (lightly doped, thick): it increases the blocking (voltage) capacity. Thicker drift gives a higher breakdown voltage but a higher on-state resistance and causes the quasi-saturation region.
  • Vertical structure: maximises the cross-section area for current, so on-resistance and thermal resistance are low.
  • Interdigitated emitter and base (many narrow emitter fingers) to reduce emitter current crowding.
  • Low β, so a Darlington connection is often used to raise the gain.

4. Breakdown: primary and second breakdown

Primary breakdown

  • Normal avalanche breakdown of the collector-base junction, with a large current.
  • BV_SUS (sustaining voltage) is the limit: the maximum v_CE the transistor can sustain while carrying collector current.
  • BV_SUS < BV_CEO < BV_CBO. BV_CEO and BV_CBO are the standoff voltages with zero or negative base current.

Second breakdown (secondary breakdown)

  • A failure mode seen as a sudden (precipitous) drop of v_CE at large currents.
  • Cause: power dissipation is not uniform across the chip but concentrated in small regions. The local temperature rises quickly, and a BJT's resistance falls as temperature rises (negative temperature coefficient). So the hot spot conducts even more current, gets hotter still: thermal runaway.
  • The hot spots can melt and recrystallise the silicon, destroying the device.
hot spot → lower resistance there → more current → more heat → hotter spot → … → melting

How to avoid second breakdown (slide)

  1. Keep power dissipation under control.
  2. Use a controlled rate of change of base current during turn-off.
  3. Use protective snubber circuits.
  4. Keep the switching trajectory within the SOA boundaries.

5. Safe operating area (SOA)

Fig. 3: (a) Forward-bias SOA with its four boundaries. (b) Reverse-bias SOA at turn-off.

FBSOA (forward-bias SOA, also simply called SOA)

  • Boundaries: I_CM (maximum current), the thermal limit (maximum power dissipation), the second-breakdown limit, and the maximum voltage BV_CEO.
  • The area expands for pulsed (switching) operation.

RBSOA (reverse-bias SOA, during turn-off)

  • Inductive loads give higher peak energy at turn-off than resistive loads, so second breakdown can happen if the RBSOA is exceeded.
  • A reverse base current expands the RBSOA.
  • Below V_CEO, the safe area does not depend on the reverse bias V_EB; it is limited only by I_C. Above V_CEO, the collector current must be controlled depending on the reverse-bias voltage.
  • Temperature derates the SOA.

Improving SOA capability

SOA is set by peak power handling, which depends on current density and thermal density. Keep both low: a wide base limits current density, and good heat sinking under the collector lets the device withstand high peak power. Spreading power over a large silicon area gives the highest SOA. Failure starts from a hot spot at the weakest point.

6. Switching characteristics

Fig. 4: Switching waveforms of a power BJT (resistive load): base current, collector current and collector-emitter voltage.
TimeMeaning
Delay time t_dFrom the base drive until i_C reaches 10%. Time to charge the base-emitter junction capacitance to forward bias. Reduced by a larger base current with a high slope.
Rise time t_ri_C rises from 10% to 90% of I_CS (index "r" = 10 to 90%).
Turn-on timet_on = t_d + t_r
Storage time t_sAfter the turn-off signal, the time needed to neutralise the minority carriers stored in the base and collector. i_C stays almost constant. The most important BJT parameter; the main limit on switching speed.
Fall time t_fi_C falls from 90% to 10%: crossing from saturation to cut-off. Reduced by a negative base current and by keeping the device in quasi-saturation (less stored charge).
Turn-off timet_off = t_s + t_f

Switching losses occur during t_r and t_f, when v_CE and i_C are both large at the same time. Storage time and switching losses are the key issues with power BJTs.

Base drive optimisation

Fig. 5: Ideal base-drive current waveform.
  • I_B1: high forward base current with a fast rise, so the BJT turns on quickly (small t_d and t_r).
  • I_B2: lower holding current that keeps the BJT in quasi-saturation: conduction loss stays low and excess base charge is avoided.
  • −I_BR with a controlled slope: a reverse base current sweeps out the stored charge, reducing t_s and t_f (and expanding the RBSOA).

7. Applications

SMPS · power amplifiers · DC-to-AC inverters · relays · power control circuits.

8. Previous-year questions with full solutions

2022 Q4(b)Q. Explain the switching characteristics of a BJT. [6]
Answer. Draw Fig. 4 (i_B, i_C and v_CE on the same time axis) and mark t_d, t_r, t_s, t_f.
  • Turn-on: when the base current I_B1 is applied, i_C does not rise at once. During the delay time t_d the base-emitter junction capacitance charges up to forward bias. Then i_C rises from 10% to 90% of I_CS during the rise time t_r, while v_CE falls to V_CE(sat). t_on = t_d + t_r.
  • Turn-off: when the base current is reversed (−I_B2), the excess minority carriers stored in the base and collector must first be removed. During this storage time t_s, i_C stays almost constant. Then i_C falls from 90% to 10% during the fall time t_f while v_CE rises to V_CC. t_off = t_s + t_f.
  • Losses: during t_r and t_f both v_CE and i_C are large, so most switching loss occurs there.
  • Improving switching: a large, fast-rising I_B1 reduces t_d and t_r; holding the device in quasi-saturation (I_B2) reduces stored charge; a negative base current reduces t_s and t_f.
2024 Q3(b)Q. What is secondary breakdown? Explain why secondary breakdown occurs in BJT and thyristors but not in MOSFETs. [7]
Secondary breakdown is a destructive failure of a power transistor at high current and voltage, seen as a sudden collapse of v_CE while the current is large. It is caused by localised thermal runaway (hot spots), not by the normal avalanche of the junction (that is primary breakdown). Mark the second-breakdown limit on the output characteristics or SOA (Fig. 2 or 3a).

Why in BJTs and thyristors:
  • They are bipolar (minority-carrier) devices. Their on-state resistance has a negative temperature coefficient: as temperature rises, more carriers are generated, so resistance falls.
  • Current is never perfectly uniform. A slightly hotter region therefore carries more current, which heats it further (current crowding / filamentation).
  • This positive feedback gives local thermal runaway; the hot spot melts and recrystallises the silicon and the device is destroyed. In thyristors the regenerative (latching) bipolar action makes the current filament worse.
Why not in MOSFETs:
  • A MOSFET is a majority-carrier (unipolar) device with no minority-carrier storage.
  • Its R_DS(on) has a positive temperature coefficient: carrier mobility falls as temperature rises, so resistance rises.
  • A hotter region therefore carries less current, and current shifts to cooler regions. The current spreads evenly over the chip (self-stabilising), so no hot spot or thermal runaway can form.
BJT: T↑ → R↓ → I↑ → T↑↑ (runaway)   |   MOSFET: T↑ → R↑ → I↓ → T stable
2024 Q1(d)Q. Explain why BJT and SCR cannot be operated in parallel, whereas MOSFETs can operate in parallel. [5]
When devices are paralleled, they must share the current equally. Whether they do depends on the temperature coefficient of their on-state resistance.
  • BJT: negative temperature coefficient (V_CE(sat) falls as temperature rises). The transistor with slightly lower V_CE(sat) takes more current, heats up, and its V_CE(sat) falls further, so it takes even more. This current hogging leads to thermal runaway of one device. Paralleling needs matched devices and emitter ballast resistors.
  • SCR: also a bipolar device with negative temperature coefficient. In addition, SCRs have different on-state voltages and different turn-on times: the first one to latch takes the whole current. Paralleling needs series resistors or inductors and simultaneous, strong gate pulses.
  • MOSFET: R_DS(on) has a positive temperature coefficient. The device carrying more current heats up, its resistance rises and its current falls. Current sharing is automatic, so MOSFETs can be paralleled easily; only matched gate drive (small gate resistors) is needed.
2023 Q6(c)Q. Mention the constructional features of a power BJT. [part of 8]
Draw Fig. 1, then list:
  1. Vertical structure n⁺-p-n⁻-n⁺: the collector is at the bottom, so the current-carrying area is large.
  2. Heavily doped n⁺ emitter (≈10¹⁹ cm⁻³, ≈10 µm), p base (≈10¹⁶, 5–20 µm).
  3. Lightly doped n⁻ collector drift region (≈10¹⁴, 50–200 µm): gives the high blocking voltage; causes quasi-saturation.
  4. n⁺ substrate (≈250 µm) forms the collector contact.
  5. Interdigitated base-emitter fingers to avoid emitter current crowding.
  6. Low current gain β (5 to 20); Darlington pairs used for higher gain.
  7. Large metal case / heat-sink mounting to dissipate heat.

9. Practice questions (likely next) with short answers

PredictedQ. What is quasi-saturation? Why does it occur only in power BJTs?
A region between the active region and hard saturation where both junctions are (just) forward biased but the lightly doped n⁻ drift region is not yet fully flooded with carriers, so v_CE is moderate. It occurs because power BJTs have the vertical n⁻ drift region; small-signal BJTs do not. Operating there reduces stored charge and speeds up turn-off.
PredictedQ. Draw and explain the FBSOA and RBSOA of a power BJT.
Draw Fig. 3. FBSOA: limited by I_CM, the thermal (P_max) line, the second-breakdown line and BV_CEO; it expands for pulsed operation. RBSOA: the turn-off limit; below V_CEO only I_C limits it; above V_CEO it depends on the reverse base bias, which expands it. Inductive turn-off is the critical case.
PredictedQ. Explain the modes of operation of a power BJT. / Explain primary breakdown and BV_SUS.
Use the table in Section 3 and Fig. 2. Primary breakdown = avalanche of the C-B junction; BV_SUS is the maximum v_CE with collector current flowing; BV_SUS < BV_CEO < BV_CBO.
PredictedQ. How can the base drive improve the switching performance of a BJT? / How can second breakdown be avoided?
Base drive: I_B1 (high, fast) for fast turn-on; I_B2 to hold quasi-saturation; −I_BR with controlled slope to remove stored charge (Fig. 5). Second breakdown: control power dissipation, control di_B/dt at turn-off, use snubbers, keep the trajectory inside the SOA.
